About this verifier

This service lets a real person show that they control a Fediverse account (for example on Mastodon), optionally under their legal name, using the government digital ID in their wallet app. The account's own server never sees the ID.

It is meant to help readers tell real people from impersonators and automated accounts, while keeping pseudonymous accounts possible and storing as little as possible about anyone.

What a proof says

LevelPublished claimAsked from your wallet
fediverse-verified name
link: /n/…		 "This account is controlled by [legal name], confirmed with an ID issued by [country (authority)]." Given name, family name, issuing country and authority
fediverse-verified person
link: /u/…		 "This account is controlled by a real person, confirmed with an ID issued by [country (authority)]." No other personal details. Only the issuing country and authority
+ 18+ (optional, with either level) "…and they are 18 or over." A yes/no "over 18" answer (how)

Readers see which government vouched for the person, so they can weigh it. For the fediverse-verified person level this reveals roughly where you're from, at country level only.

A proof says nothing about whether someone has other accounts: one person may verify several. Proofs don't expire; they last until removed.

Showing you're 18 or over

If you choose to add "18+", we ask your wallet for the EU proof-of-age credential, which exists for exactly this: it answers only "is this person over 18?" and contains no name, birth date or ID number (EU Age Verification specification).

The Swiss e-ID answers "over 18?" directly, so no birth date is ever involved there.

For EU wallets at the fediverse-verified name level only, if you have no proof-of-age credential, we may instead ask for your birth date from your ID. It is used once to work out whether you're 18 or over and then discarded: it's never stored or published. Your name is published at that level anyway; for fediverse-verified person we never ask for a birth date.

If your wallet doesn't confirm you're 18 or over, the proof is issued without mentioning age, and nothing about your age is kept. "18+" reflects a check at the time of verification of whoever controlled the account then; services with legal age-check duties may still need their own.

How it works

  1. You confirm the account is yours, either by linking to your proof from your profile (we then check your public profile), or by signing in with Mastodon (your server lets us read your account details once, then we cancel that permission).
  2. Your wallet shares only what the chosen level needs. This works with {{ wallets | map(attribute='label') | join(' and ') }}. The request uses OpenID for Verifiable Presentations; your wallet shows exactly what is asked and you approve it. The credential's signature is checked against the issuing government's keys.
  3. We publish a proof page that links back to your profile with rel="me". When your profile links to the page, Mastodon shows a green check mark next to that link.
  4. The page also carries a signed copy of the proof's details, so that anyone, including Fediverse servers that choose to trust this verifier, can check for themselves that it came from us and hasn't been altered.

The signed copy

Every proof page contains a copy of the proof's details that this verifier has digitally signed. A signature works like a tamper-evident seal: using our public key, anyone can check that the copy really was issued here and that not a single character has been changed since. That lets other services rely on a proof without having to trust the web page around it.

It holds exactly the facts shown on the proof page, nothing more:

"account_ownership_check"link-backHow the account was proven yours (or mastodon-sign-in)
"iat"1792500000When it was issued, in seconds since 1970
"jti"6q3yJY3bcSvJOlvkThe proof's ID, as in its link

The signed copy is published in two forms carrying the same facts:

  • A JSON Web Token (ES256), shown above: easy to check with standard JWT tools and our public keys. Download it by adding .jwt to the proof link.
  • A W3C Verifiable Credential with a Data Integrity proof (eddsa-jcs-2022), issued by this verifier's ActivityPub actor. Mastodon already checks this kind of signature for Fediverse messages, so Fediverse servers can check proofs with code they already have. Download it by adding .json to the proof link.

The short names iss, sub, iat and jti are fixed by the JSON Web Token standard, name follows OpenID Connect, and the issuer fields use the EU PID rulebook's names. Technically it's a JSON Web Token signed with ES256. It's in the proof page's source, and each proof page has a "What's in the signed copy?" section showing its contents. You can also download it by adding .jwt to the proof link.

"Sign in with Mastodon": what we receive

This uses OAuth, the standard behind "Sign in with…" buttons. You type your password only into your own Mastodon server; we never see it.

  1. Your server asks you to approve "{{ settings.app_name }}" with one permission, read:accounts: reading your account details (name, handle, profile). It cannot post, read your private messages or notifications, follow anyone, or change any setting.
  2. When you approve, your server gives us a one-time access token for that permission. We use it for a single request, to read your own account details, which tells us the account is yours.
  3. We then immediately ask your server to cancel the token. From then on it no longer works.

The token exists only in this service's memory during that one sign-in request, typically less than a second, and is never written to disk, the database or any log. It is not your password or a login session: even while it exists, it can't be used to log in as you or act as you.

If your server doesn't confirm the cancellation (for example because of a network error), we tell you straight away, with a link to remove "{{ settings.app_name }}" from your server's authorized apps yourself. You can always see and remove it yourself in your Mastodon settings under Account → Authorized apps.

Keys, signatures and official registration

Signatures use a key pair. The private key is kept secret on this server and is the only thing that can create our signatures. The matching public key is published so that anyone can check them. If someone altered a proof, or made a fake one, the check against our public key would fail.

We sign with two keys, one per form of the signed copy. The JWT key has the ID {{ signing_key_id }} (ES256, curve P-256). The credential key is an Ed25519 key published on our ActivityPub actor as /actor#ed25519-key. The ID is a fingerprint of the public key (RFC 7638), so you can compare it with what other services show for a proof signed here.

What we store, and what we don't

Stored, per proofNever stored
  • Your Fediverse handle (e.g. @alice@example.social) and the web links to your account and its profile page
  • The level, the date, and how you confirmed the account
  • Your legal name, only for the fediverse-verified name level
  • The country and authority that issued your ID

  • A one-way hash of your removal code (not the code itself)

Exactly what is stored, and how

This service keeps its data in one SQLite database file, verifier.sqlite3, plus one key file, both in its data folder on the server's disk. Nothing else is written to disk.

Deleting

Removing a proof deletes its row outright, and the database runs with secure_delete on, so the deleted content is overwritten with zeros rather than left in the file. Pending proofs are deleted the same way after {{ pending_hours }} hours. This software makes no backups; if the operator backs up the data folder, removed proofs remain in those backups until the backups themselves are deleted.

Held only briefly, never on disk

  • In this service's memory (gone when it restarts): your session while you verify or sign in, for up to {{ flow_minutes }} minutes (your account, the chosen level and the wallet request ID; in test mode, also the details typed into the mock wallet until they're read), and the time of the last check of each pending proof.
  • In the EU verifier component, which talks to your wallet (used only with real wallets, not in test mode): the wallet's response (only the details requested above), in memory, for at most about 40 minutes, after which its clean-up deletes it.
  • In your browser: a signed (not encrypted) session cookie, fv_session, holding your session ID, and the new proof's ID and removal code until the "proof is ready" page has been shown once. It stays on your device.
  • Logs: request logging is turned off, so visits, IP addresses and proof IDs are not logged by this service. Only errors are logged, to the server console.

Published means public

A proof page is public by design: anyone can read it while it exists, and Fediverse servers fetch it to check your profile link. Others may therefore keep copies, for example servers that cached your profile, archives or people who saved the page. Removing a proof deletes it from this server, but cannot reach copies made elsewhere. Please keep this in mind before publishing your legal name.

{% if settings.storage_encrypted %}

The data folder is on an encrypted disk whose passphrase is entered by hand after every restart. That mainly protects the keys that identify this verifier; for the published proofs themselves, what matters is that removal really deletes them.

{% else %}

The data folder is not on an encrypted disk on this server. Published proofs are public anyway; what matters for them is that removal really deletes them. The signing key is readable only by the verifier.

{% endif %}

Removing your data

You can remove a proof at any time with the removal code you were given, or by signing in with your account. Removal deletes everything we hold about that proof, including any name; the page then no longer exists. This is your right to erasure under GDPR (Article 17).

Also remove the link from your profile: your own server keeps showing its green check mark until you do. Other servers drop it when they next refresh your profile.

Standards used

OpenID for Verifiable Presentations 1.0
How we ask your wallet for specific details (using its DCQL query language) and receive them, for both EU wallets and the Swiss e-ID.
RFC 9901: Selective Disclosure for JWTs (SD-JWT) and SD-JWT VC
The credential format that lets your wallet reveal only some details, such as your name but not your birth date. Used by both EU wallets and the Swiss e-ID.
Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 (eIDAS 2.0), Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2977
The EU law behind the European Digital Identity Wallet and the person identification data (PID) it carries.
EUDI Wallet Architecture and Reference Framework, PID Rulebook
The technical framework for EU wallets and the exact PID attributes we may request.
IT-Wallet technical specifications
Italy's national profile of the EU wallet (the IO app).
swiyu, the Swiss e-ID trust infrastructure: Swiss Profile Verification and Swiss Profile Trust
Switzerland's e-ID: the same request protocol (OpenID4VP with DCQL) and credential format (SD-JWT VC), with issuers and verifiers identified by DIDs in federal registries instead of EU certificates.
W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and did:webvh
How Swiss e-ID issuers and verifiers are identified: each has a DID whose keys and history are published and checked against the swiyu registries.
EU Age Verification specification and ISO/IEC 18013-5 (mdoc)
For "18+" with EU wallets: the proof-of-age credential, in the mdoc format from the mobile driving licence standard, which answers only "over 18?".
rel="me" and Mastodon profile verification
The two-way link that makes Mastodon show a green check mark.
ActivityPub and RFC 7033: WebFinger
How we find your account and read your public profile, on any Fediverse server.
OAuth 2.0, PKCE, token revocation
"Sign in with Mastodon", with the token revoked straight after use.
JWT, JWS, JWA (ES256), JWK, JWK thumbprint
The format of the signed copy on each proof page, and of the public keys used to check it.
W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0, Data Integrity EdDSA (eddsa-jcs-2022) and RFC 8785: JSON Canonicalization
The second form of the signed copy: a standard credential, signed in the format Mastodon already verifies.
FEP-8b32: Object Integrity Proofs and FEP-521a: Representing actor's public keys
How Fediverse software signs and checks objects, and finds the signing key on an actor; the credential key is published this way.
FEP-c390: Identity Proofs
Related Fediverse proposal for linking identities to accounts; this verifier aims to align with Fediverse Enhancement Proposals over time.
